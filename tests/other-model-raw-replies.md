## A1

> Placeholder text disappears as soon as someone starts typing, so it's not a reliable label. This creates a barrier for several groups of people:
>
> - People who use screen readers cannot hear what each field is for, because placeholder text isn't announced as a label.
> - People with cognitive disabilities or older adults may lose track of what they're entering once the placeholder disappears.
> - People with low vision or on small screens may not see the placeholder text at all.
>
> **What to do:**
>
> Add visible labels to every form field. These should be on the page at all times, not just in the placeholder. Keep the placeholder text as optional helper text that describes format or examples—for example, a placeholder like "MM/DD/YYYY" is fine if you also have a visible label that says "Expiration date."
>
> **Who handles this:**
>
> - Designers should create layouts with space for persistent labels.
> - Developers should associate each label with its form field using proper HTML or ARIA, so screen readers understand the connection.
>
> **Learn more:**
>
> See Understanding WCAG 2.2 for specific guidance: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/labels-or-instructions.html
>
> This applies to your form regardless of which fields it has—payment card details, shipping address, all of it.

## A6

> **Keyboard access for dropdowns is essential for people who cannot use a mouse.** This includes people with motor disabilities, people using assistive technologies like screen readers, and power users who navigate with keyboard only.
>
> If your dropdown works only with a mouse (by clicking to open and close), keyboard users are blocked from using that feature.
>
> Here's what keyboard access means for a dropdown:
>
> - Users can reach it with Tab
> - Users can open the dropdown with Enter or Space
> - Users can move between options with arrow keys (up and down)
> - Users can select an option and close the dropdown
> - Users can press Escape to close without selecting
> - The currently selected option is visible with a clear focus indicator
>
> **If you're building the dropdown from scratch, use a native HTML `<select>` element.** It provides all of this behavior automatically across browsers and assistive technologies. Development team handles this.
>
> **If you're using a custom dropdown component,** your development team needs to:
> - Trap focus when the dropdown is open (arrow keys navigate the list, not the page)
> - Announce which option is selected to people using screen readers
> - Allow keyboard selection with Enter or Space
> - Close on Escape without making a selection
> - Show a visible focus indicator on each option
>
> Learn more about keyboard patterns for menus and dropdowns: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/quickref/#keyboard

## C1

> Blind users access your site through a screen reader. This is assistive technology that reads the page content aloud and helps users navigate using the keyboard.
>
> Here's what they experience:
>
> **How they navigate.** Blind users don't click the mouse. They use the keyboard to move through your site—pressing Tab to jump to links and buttons, using arrow keys in menus, and activating controls by pressing Enter or Space. They often jump between headings or skip to specific landmarks (like the main content area) to find what they need quickly.
>
> **What they hear.** The screen reader announces the structure of your page out loud: headings, links, form labels, buttons, and other landmarks. If your page lacks clear structure—missing headings, unlabeled buttons, or images with no text descriptions—the user hears very little context about what they can do.
>
> **What they can't see.** Blind users can't see visual design, color, layout, or images. If your site relies on those to communicate meaning, blind users miss that information. For example, a red asterisk to mark required form fields won't help—you need a text label saying "required."
>
> **What helps them.** Good page structure (proper heading hierarchy), descriptive link and button names, form labels clearly connected to their fields, and text alternatives for images all make navigation faster and clearer.
>
> To discuss specific barriers on your site, could you tell me which page or task you'd like to improve? That will help me give you concrete steps for designers or developers.

## C3

> Focus indicators matter because they show which part of a web page is active and ready for input.
>
> A focus indicator is a mark or highlight. It appears when you press Tab or use other keyboard keys to move around a page. It shows you where you are now.
>
> **Who needs focus indicators?**
>
> Many people use keyboards instead of a mouse. They might have limited hand control, or they prefer keyboards. People who use screen readers—software that reads text aloud—also depend on focus indicators. They need to know where they are on the page.
>
> **Why do they matter?**
>
> Without a focus indicator, keyboard users cannot tell which button or link is active. They might click the wrong thing by mistake. On some pages, the focus indicator is hidden or invisible. This makes the page very hard to use.
>
> **What happens when focus indicators are clear?**
>
> When focus indicators are visible and easy to see, keyboard users can navigate quickly and correctly. They know exactly where they are. They can complete tasks like filling in forms or clicking links without confusion.
>
> **Learn more**
>
> For technical details and rules about this, read about Focus Visible at the W3C: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-visible.html

## C2 (turn 2: "Explain why captions matter for our users.")

> Captions make video and audio content accessible to the Deaf community. Without captions, your users cannot access the information, dialogue, or context in videos, podcasts, or live presentations.
>
> When captions are present, the Deaf community can read what's being said and understand important sound effects, music cues, and speaker changes. This is how they participate equally in video content.
>
> For prerecorded videos, captions should include all dialogue and relevant sound descriptions. For live video or audio (like webinars or broadcasts), real-time captions let your users follow along as it happens. Without this, video content becomes a barrier that excludes them entirely.
>
> Captions are not a nice-to-have feature—they are essential for the Deaf community to use your product. Including them means everyone can access the same information.
>
> Learn more about caption requirements and techniques in the [Understanding WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/) guidance.
